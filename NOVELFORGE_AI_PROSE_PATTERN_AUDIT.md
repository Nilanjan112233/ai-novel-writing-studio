# NovelForge AI Prose Pattern Audit

## Verdict

The prose still reads AI-written in meaningful portions, especially after Chapter 1. It is not bad grammar. The issue is patterned storytelling: generalized emotional explanation, repeated sentence shapes, recurring abstract nouns, symmetrical lists, and endings that announce a larger mystery instead of allowing a scene's consequence to land.

Chapter 1 contains enough concrete river and infrastructure detail to disguise the pattern at first. By Chapters 7–10, the voice becomes easier to identify as generated because the same escalation grammar and abstract terminology recur without enough distinctive dialogue or social texture.

## Observed patterns

### Repeated openings and sentence shapes

- Many chapters begin with “The …” followed by a physical condition or environmental statement.
- Several paragraphs use a sensory cue, an explanation of what it means, then a sentence that widens the mystery.
- “She did not …” and “She only knew …” constructions recur, often stating the emotional interpretation after the image already showed it.
- “It was …” and “There was …” carry abstract conclusions that could be replaced by observable behavior.
- Em-dash or `---` breaks create the same dramatic pause across chapters.

### Repeated abstractions

The recurring nouns are “seam,” “cost,” “memory,” “choice,” “weight,” “voice,” “fault,” “claim,” and “truth.” These are appropriate story ideas, but they are often used as explanations rather than as specific sensory or relational events.

### Explanatory triads

The prose repeatedly groups three abstract or emotional terms together, for example a character noticing danger, cost, and consequence, or a mystery being described as gift, wound, or claim. The pattern is persuasive in a planning document but conspicuous in fiction.

### Hook-template endings

Later chapters often end with one of these forms:

1. a new seam or anomaly appears;
2. a voice or figure proves the mystery is larger;
3. a countdown is stated;
4. the protagonist reaches toward the unknown;
5. the scene cuts before a consequence occurs.

This keeps pages turning but makes the story feel engineered around cliffhangers rather than driven by changing relationships.

### Summary-like interiority

The text sometimes says what the protagonist feels or understands in a clean abstract sentence instead of allowing the reader to infer it from a failed action, a physical habit, a lie, a social mistake, or a specific memory.

### Dialogue flattening

Dialogue is functional and mostly delivers information or confirms stakes. Characters do not yet have enough individual rhythm, evasion, humor, status behavior, or disagreement. This is one reason the generated material feels like a plan expanded into scenes.

## Chapter trend

| Range | AI-pattern visibility | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ch1 opening | Low to medium | Concrete levy work and sensory river detail are specific |
| Ch1 backstory/power section | Medium | Dossier-like explanation and abstract memory framing |
| Ch2 | High | Continuity failure makes the prose feel like an independent generated setup |
| Ch3–5 | Medium | Concrete events return, but endings and emotional explanations repeat |
| Ch6–8 | Medium to high | System/lore vocabulary expands and becomes self-referential |
| Ch9–10 | High | Repeated seam/voice/threshold structures and generalized interiority |

## Why ordinary humanization is not enough

Changing synonyms or smoothing sentences would not address the main problem. The prose needs scene-level revision:

- replace stated emotions with choices and social friction;
- make the old-life reflex produce both useful and harmful behavior;
- give each supporting character a conflicting want;
- vary chapter endings so some end on decisions, losses, discoveries, or changed relationships;
- make every system term earn its meaning in a concrete event;
- cut repeated abstract explanation after the reader has understood the idea.

## What the tested revision did

The successful selected rewrite changed:

`Renn's hands remembered things her mouth couldn't explain.`

to:

`Renn's fingers knew the cold of a different river, the vibration of a different wall under a different sky—knowledge that her mouth could not name.`

This is a real local improvement: it replaces “things” with concrete sensory imagery and keeps the old-life/second-world distinction. It is still a sentence-level improvement, not proof that the whole chapter has been humanized. The failed whole-chapter revision confirms that the broader path needs reliability and probably stronger revision constraints.

## Recommended literary quality gate

Before calling a revised chapter humanized, review it blind against the source for:

- changed scene purpose, not only wording;
- specific imagery and sensory continuity;
- character-specific behavior and dialogue;
- fewer abstract explanations;
- varied openings and endings;
- preserved facts, chronology, POV, and protected images;
- no new lore or invented relationships.
